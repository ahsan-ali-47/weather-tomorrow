    Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade Postponed as Storm Hits New York

    The 100th parade will now march on Saturday, after a coastal storm made Thursday too dangerous for the giant balloons.

    Why the wind limits exist, how strong the gusts will be — and what the wider storm is doing across America.

    By Ahsan Ali, Founder, Weather Tomorrow · 26 November 2026

    Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade Postponed: What Happened

    On Wednesday 25 November, the day before Thanksgiving, organisers made a decision the parade had never needed in a century: move it. The 100th Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade, which was due to step off in New York City on Thanksgiving morning, Thursday 26 November, will now take place on Saturday 28 November, starting at 8:30 a.m. The decision was taken jointly by Macy's, New York City officials and the broadcaster NBC, and the reason given was safety — for the people taking part and for the crowds watching from the street.

    The warning signs had been building all week. A powerful coastal storm, sometimes called a nor'easter, was crawling up the East Coast toward New York, carrying heavy rain, strong winds and coastal flooding with it. Earlier in the week, the main question was whether the parade's giant balloons could fly. By Wednesday, the forecast had hardened into something more serious: gusts strong enough to endanger not just the balloons, but anyone standing in an open street. The balloons had already been inflated on the Upper West Side on 25 November, in preparation for the parade. After the postponement was announced, workers deflated them again. The images of balloons being let down, still under their protective netting, told the story more plainly than any forecast map: the weather had won this round, and the parade would wait for a calmer day.

    That matters because of what this parade is. It dates back to 1924, and the 2026 edition was planned as a milestone — a 100th parade, with a record 100 inflatable elements, floats, marching bands, performers and the traditional appearance by Santa Claus at the end. It had been cancelled before, for three years during the Second World War when helium was in short supply, but it had never been postponed and moved to another day because of weather. A first in a hundred years is not a decision organisers take lightly. It is also a measure of how serious this storm is. When an event that has survived a century of November weather, including rain and cold, is moved for the first time, the forecast behind that choice deserves to be taken seriously by everyone else in the storm's path too.

    Why the Parade Balloons Cannot Fly in Strong Wind

    The balloons are the heart of the parade, and they are also its weakest point in wind. Each one is a huge helium-filled shape held down by teams of handlers with ropes. In calm air that works well. In a gust, a balloon stops behaving like a decoration and starts behaving like a sail: the wind catches its full surface, the handlers feel a sudden pull, and the balloon can surge sideways, dip toward the street, or drag the people holding it. That is why New York City sets a hard limit. The giant balloons are not allowed to fly when sustained winds pass 23 mph, or when gusts reach 34 mph or more. The limit is not about the balloons looking untidy. It is about keeping a very large object under control above a very crowded street.

    What Wind Speeds Are Forecast for Thanksgiving Day?

    Thursday's forecast sits well above that limit. Reports put wind gusts at 40 to 60 mph in parts of the region, with stronger gusts possible along exposed coastal areas, and New York City's mayor said gusts of up to 65 mph were possible in the city, along with potential flash flooding. High wind warnings were in place for areas including Long Island, New York City, Philadelphia and Norfolk, Virginia, with gusts of 60 mph or higher possible. At those speeds, the question is no longer whether the balloons could be flown low or held back. The whole parade route — handlers, marching bands, floats, barriers and spectators — would be standing in winds that can knock over unsecured objects and bring down branches. Moving the parade was not only about saving the balloons. It was about not putting thousands of people in the street on the worst day of the storm.

    Why Postpone the Whole Parade Instead of Grounding the Balloons?

    It is a fair question, because in past years balloons have sometimes been kept low or grounded while the rest of the parade went ahead. The difference this time is the scale of the storm. Heavy rain, damaging gusts and coastal flooding were all forecast for the same morning, and a state of emergency was declared for New York City and the nearby counties of Nassau, Suffolk and Westchester. A parade is not just a television show. It closes streets, fills viewing areas with crowds who have often waited for hours, and needs police, medical cover and transport to work normally around it. In a storm that is expected to flood low-lying roads and slow travel across the region, asking crowds to gather and stand in it would add risk for no good reason. Postponing the whole event, rather than running a smaller version in dangerous weather, keeps the celebration intact for a day when people can actually enjoy it. Saturday's forecast is much more favourable, which is exactly why that day was chosen.

    New York Storm Forecast: Wind, Rain and Coastal Flooding

    The storm hitting New York on Thanksgiving Day is a coastal low that has been strengthening as it moves north along the Eastern Seaboard. It brings a familiar but dangerous combination: onshore winds pushing water toward the coast, heavy rain falling on ground that is already wet, and tides running high. The heaviest rain is expected to spread from the Southeast through the Mid-Atlantic and into New England, with 1 to 3 inches across parts of the Mid-Atlantic and New England and higher totals in places. Thursday is the peak day of impact for much of the Northeast, with the storm reaching New England by the afternoon or evening, and showers and wind lingering into Friday before the heaviest rain moves out of northern New England later in the day.

    Coastal flooding is the part of this storm that reaches beyond the rain gauge. The storm is arriving on top of already high astronomical tides, at the time of this week's full moon, which increases the risk of flooding in low-lying coastal communities. Moderate to major coastal flooding was already happening along the coasts of Georgia and the Carolinas on Wednesday, with Charleston's tide gauge passing 8 feet at high tide and spending almost two hours at major flood stage, flooding roads and closing entrances to coastal sites. The threat then stretches north to Long Island and coastal New England, with the highest water expected around the Thanksgiving morning high tide in North Carolina, the Virginia Tidewater, Delaware, the Jersey Shore and Long Island, and around midday on Thursday in coastal New England, possibly again on Friday. For anyone near the coast, the advice is the same as in any coastal flood: water on a road can be deeper and faster than it looks, and a closed road should be treated as a warning, not a suggestion.

    Check the live forecast for the city before you travel. The New York weather page shows the current conditions, wind and rain outlook for the city, and the Philadelphia weather page, the Boston weather page and the Washington DC weather page cover the other big cities along the storm's track. Forecasts can shift as the storm moves, and a small change in its track changes which places get the worst wind and rain, so a check on the morning of travel is worth more than a forecast read two days earlier.

    Thanksgiving Storm Across America: Snow, Rain and Record Flooding

    New York is the headline, but this is a coast-to-coast Thanksgiving storm, and its effects are very different depending on where you are. In the Midwest, the same system is bringing heavy, wet snow. Moderate snow was already falling in parts of Missouri and Illinois on Wednesday night, and up to 5 inches of snow was possible around St. Louis, with snow or a wet rain-and-snow mix expected across the Midwest and into parts of Tennessee on Thanksgiving Day. Wet snow is heavy snow: it sticks to trees and power lines, it turns roads slushy and then icy, and it makes driving slow and tiring. As colder air wraps into the storm on Friday, the chance of a rain-and-snow mix spreads across much of the Ohio Valley. For drivers crossing the interior, the danger is not a blizzard so much as a long, wet, slippery journey with poor visibility.

    In the Southeast and Florida, the story is rain — and a lot of it. South Florida recorded remarkable totals on the eve of Thanksgiving. At Marathon in the Florida Keys, 6.90 inches of rain had fallen by 7:30 p.m. on Wednesday, according to the National Weather Service in Key West, making it the wettest November day on record there and the seventh-wettest day of any month since records began in 1950. The previous daily record was only 2.56 inches, set in 2009, and the previous wettest November day was 6.68 inches on 15 November 2023. Miami recorded 4.02 inches by 4 p.m. on Wednesday, breaking its daily record of 2.37 inches from 2009, and the total later reached 4.52 inches, making it Miami's 10th-wettest November day in records going back to 1895. West Palm Beach received nearly 5 inches on Wednesday alone, taking its monthly total to 23.29 inches and shattering its previous November record of 14.63 inches, while Fort Lauderdale had its wettest November on record, with more than 15 inches through the previous week. These are not just big numbers. In flat, low-lying Florida, several inches of rain in a day means flooded streets, closed underpasses and standing water that stays long after the rain stops. The Miami weather page and the Orlando weather page show the live outlook for Florida's cities.

    Put together, forecasters warned that dangerous weather threatened more than 25 states, with heavy rain, damaging winds, snow and coastal flooding stretching from coast to coast, and around 81.5 million Americans were expected to travel for Thanksgiving. That is why the delays matter so much this week. Aviation forecasters expected reduced visibility, turbulence, strong crosswinds and icing conditions from the Carolinas northward through Friday morning, all of which slow arrivals and departures even when runways stay open. On the West Coast, exceptionally high king tides and large waves continued to fuel coastal flooding, particularly around the San Francisco Bay Area. The parade postponement, the state of emergency, the flooded roads in the Carolinas and the record rain in Florida are not separate stories. They are the same storm system, and the same week of very high tides, showing up in different ways in different places.

    When Will the Parade Happen Now? Saturday 28 November

    The rescheduled parade is planned for Saturday 28 November, starting at 8:30 a.m., and it is expected to air on NBC and Peacock. For viewers who still want a parade on Thanksgiving morning, NBC will show a replay of the 2025 parade at 8:30 a.m. in all time zones. Some details for Saturday were still being worked through when the postponement was announced, including updated street closures, public viewing arrangements and broadcast details, and Macy's said its team was working through the operational logistics and would share details as they were finalised. That is normal for a move made at short notice: the route is the same, but the road closures, staffing and crowd plans all have to be rebuilt for a different day, and a Saturday parade brings a different crowd — and different traffic — from a Thursday holiday morning.

    Saturday also looks like a genuinely better weather day, which is the point of the move. The storm is expected to move out of the Northeast during Friday, with showers and wind lingering before the heaviest rain clears northern New England later in the day. By Saturday, the wind that made Thursday unsafe should have eased, and the city can hold the celebration it planned for its 100th parade without asking handlers to fight gusts or spectators to stand in driving rain. That does not mean Saturday will be perfect — late November in New York is cold, and anyone going should dress for it and check the forecast again on the day — but the difference between a storm day and the day after a storm is often the difference between a dangerous event and a normal one. The organisers chose the calmer day. It is the same advice that applies to travel this week: where you can choose your day, choose the calmer one.

    How to Stay Safe in the Thanksgiving Storm

    If you are travelling this week, the most useful habit is the simplest one: check the forecast for the exact place and the exact hours you will be travelling, and check it again before you leave. This storm is moving, and its worst wind, rain and snow move with it. Allow extra time for every journey, because delays at airports and on highways are already happening, and a journey that is normally straightforward can take much longer in rain, snow or standing water. Keep your phone charged, carry water and warm clothing if you are driving a long distance, and tell someone your route and expected arrival time if you are driving through snow or flood-prone country. If your flight is delayed, rebook early rather than waiting at the airport for a departure time that keeps slipping, and keep an eye on your airline's app as well as the departure board.

    On the road, the two rules that save the most lives in storms like this are both simple. First, never drive through flooded roads. Water that looks shallow can hide a washed-out road surface, and a few inches of fast-moving water can carry a car away. Turn around and find another route, even if it is longer. Second, slow down and increase your following distance in rain and snow, and be ready for sudden gusts on bridges and exposed roads, where wind is stronger and there is nothing to shelter behind. If visibility drops in heavy rain or snow, pull over somewhere safe and wait for the worst of it to pass rather than pressing on. At the coast, stay away from beaches, piers and low-lying roads during high tide and the storm surge, and follow local warnings about closures. And if you are in New York on Saturday for the rescheduled parade, arrive early, dress warmly, and follow the instructions of police and parade staff — the balloon handlers will be glad of a calm day, and so will everyone watching.

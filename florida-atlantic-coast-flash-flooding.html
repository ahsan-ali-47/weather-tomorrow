    Florida's Atlantic Coast Faces Dangerous Flash Flooding

    Nonstop rain, record river levels, water rescues and emergency declarations — Florida's Atlantic coast is flooding again, and the rain is not finished yet.

    What is happening, why it keeps raining — and how to stay safe.

    By Ahsan Ali, Founder, Weather Tomorrow · 21 November 2026

    Florida Flooding: What Is Happening on the Atlantic Coast

    Florida's Atlantic coast is flooding again, and this time the rain has barely stopped for days. A slow-moving storm system has parked itself over the region, and steady easterly winds keep pushing moist air from the ocean onto the land. The result is round after round of heavy rain: roads turned into canals, neighbourhoods cut off by water, and rescue crews working through the week to pull people out of flooded homes and cars. Millions of people across the state are affected, and forecasters say the dangerous flash flood threat will continue into Saturday.

    What makes this event so serious is not just the rain itself, but what came before it. Parts of Florida are still recovering from deadly, historic flooding along the Space Coast earlier this month. Rivers were already high, the ground was already soaked, and drains were already full. When the new rain arrived, there was nowhere for the water to go. That is why a storm that might have been manageable in a dry week has turned into a state-wide emergency.

    Brevard County Flooding: Cocoa, Rockledge and a Record River

    The worst of the flooding is concentrated in Brevard County, around the towns of Cocoa, Rockledge and Merritt Island. Video from the area shows streets completely under water, with houses surrounded and driveways disappearing under the flood. The Brevard County Sheriff's Office says water rescues are still going on, and law enforcement crews have been pumping water into a nearby canal to stop more homes from flooding.

    The biggest danger in Brevard County is the St. Johns River. The river was already at major flood stage after the flooding earlier this month, and the new rain has pushed it to a record level near Cocoa — the highest it has ever been there. Communities along the river are facing the most dangerous conditions anywhere in Florida right now, because a river at record level does not just flood the banks: it keeps rising as rain keeps falling upstream, and it stays high long after the rain stops.

    The county opened a shelter on Thursday morning at the Walter Butler Community Center for people forced out of their homes, and another shelter in Melbourne in partnership with the American Red Cross. People were asked to bring their own bedding and supplies. In Palm Bay, the city ordered mandatory evacuations for residents of Palm Bay Estates, between the canal and the railroad tracks. Widespread road closures have been reported in Melbourne, West Melbourne and Palm Bay, and the Melbourne Police Department has warned drivers to stay off flooded roads.

    South Florida Flooding: Fort Lauderdale, Hollywood and Miami-Dade

    Further south, the flooding began on Tuesday night in Fort Lauderdale, Hollywood and parts of Miami-Dade County. By Wednesday, Broward County and the cities of Fort Lauderdale and Hollywood had declared states of emergency, and Florida's governor expanded an earlier emergency declaration to include Broward County and additional counties facing renewed flooding.

    The pictures from Hollywood are hard to forget. Aerial photographs from 18 November show whole neighbourhoods under water, with residents paddling kayaks down streets that used to carry cars. Wildlife officers evacuated residents and their pets from flooded homes, and in one rescue, an officer carried an infant who had stopped breathing to emergency crews after the family's vehicle became stuck in floodwater. In another rescue, more than 90 people were helped out of a flooded apartment complex in Broward County.

    The damage is not only about water in the streets. On Wednesday, the roof of a Hollywood synagogue, Temple Sinai, partly collapsed during the storms. Nobody was inside the sanctuary at the time and nobody was hurt, but the building was declared unsafe because more of the roof could give way as further rain falls. It is a reminder that days of heavy rain weaken structures as well as flood roads — a building that survived decades of hurricanes can still fail under a roof full of standing water.

    Schools across the region have also shut down. Broward County schools cancelled classes for the entire week, while schools in Brevard, Volusia, Indian River and Miami-Dade counties cancelled classes on Thursday. Several universities closed too, including Florida International University, Miami Dade College and Broward College, after severe flooding and hazardous road conditions made travel unsafe.

    Why the Rain Will Not Stop

    The reason this rain is so relentless is the shape of the weather pattern. A stalled storm system is sitting over the region instead of moving on, and easterly winds are funnelling a steady stream of moist ocean air straight into Florida's Atlantic coast. Every time one band of rain passes, another one forms behind it. Forecasters expect the repeated downpours to continue through Saturday.

    The US National Weather Service's Weather Prediction Center has put the region under a HIGH risk of excessive rainfall — a category that means there is a 70 percent or higher chance of flooding rain. That is the highest level of the centre's risk scale, and it is rarely used. It tells you how confident forecasters are: this is not a maybe, it is a near-certainty that more flooding rain is coming.

    There is also something else to watch. Forecasters are keeping an eye on a coastal storm brewing off the Southeast this weekend, which could develop into a future subtropical storm called Kyle. Even if it stays offshore, it could push more moisture and rough weather toward the Florida coast. For now, the message is the same: the rain is not over, and anyone in the affected areas should expect more of it.

    Emergency Response: National Guard, Rescues and Shelters

    Florida has mobilised a large response. On Thursday, Governor Ron DeSantis announced that hundreds of National Guard members and other state first responders had been called up, along with 50 high-water vehicles, several airboats and swamp buggies. More than 200 highway patrol troopers, 235 wildlife officers, 60 pumps and 750 road crews are supporting the effort, and the state has pre-staged more than 6,000 feet of flood control devices, with shelters on standby and extra generators and vacuum trucks ready to deploy.

    That scale of response is unusual for a rain event, and it shows how seriously officials are taking it. The wildlife officers alone have been involved in dozens of rescues, from apartment blocks to stranded drivers. But no response force can be everywhere at once, which is why the official advice is to avoid flooded areas entirely rather than rely on being rescued.

    How to Stay Safe in a Flash Flood

    Flash flooding kills more people in the United States than any other weather hazard except heat, and most of those deaths happen in cars. The rules are simple, and they save lives. First, never drive through flood water. Even shallow-looking water can hide a washed-out road, a strong current or debris, and it only takes a small depth of moving water to sweep a car away. If you meet a flooded road, turn around and find another way — no journey is worth the risk.

    Second, if water starts entering your home, move to higher ground and do not wait to gather belongings. Call for help and stay where rescuers can reach you. Third, keep your phone charged so you can receive alerts and check the hourly forecast, and follow the guidance of local officials: if your area is under a mandatory evacuation order, leave while the roads are still passable. Finally, stay out of flood water on foot as well. It can carry sewage, chemicals and hidden hazards, and it can be deeper than it looks.

    Florida Rain Forecast: What Happens Next

    The rain is expected to continue through Saturday, with the heaviest downpours along the Atlantic coast from Brevard County south to Miami-Dade. After that, the stalled system should finally begin to move, but the flooding will not end when the rain does. Rivers like the St. Johns will stay high for days, and water trapped in low-lying neighbourhoods drains slowly. Anyone returning to a flooded home should wait for the all-clear from local officials, and should never enter a building with standing water inside until it has been checked.

    For people in the affected areas, the best habit this week is a morning forecast check. Stay safe, stay dry, and let the water go down before you go out.

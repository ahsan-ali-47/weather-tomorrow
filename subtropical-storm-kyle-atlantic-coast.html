    Subtropical Storm Kyle Looms Over the Atlantic Coast

    A coastal storm off the Southeast could become Subtropical Storm Kyle, bringing heavy rain, 40 mph gusts and flash flooding from Florida to Maine.

    What is happening, who is affected — and how Thanksgiving travel could be disrupted.

    By Ahsan Ali, Founder, Weather Tomorrow · 21 November 2026

    A Coastal Storm Is Taking Shape Off the Southeast

    A new coastal storm is building off the southeastern United States, and it arrives at the worst possible moment: the weekend when millions of Americans start travelling for Thanksgiving. A centre of low pressure that formed over Florida is sliding out over the Atlantic, and forecasters expect it to strengthen through Saturday as it sits off the Southeast coast. The system is already delivering heavy rain, and computer models have grown more confident that it will hug the coastline as it moves north.

    The National Hurricane Center is watching the same system for tropical development. It has given the disturbance a medium chance of becoming a subtropical or tropical storm, and if it earns a name it will be called Kyle, the next name on this season's Atlantic list. Hurricane-hunter aircraft began flying into the system on Friday afternoon to measure its winds and structure, because where exactly the centre forms will decide whether it takes on tropical characteristics. What is unusual is the calendar: no named storm has ever formed in this stretch of ocean and tracked up the coast this late in November.

    Even if Kyle never gets named, the weather will be serious. The storm does not need a tropical title to flood roads, erode beaches and snarl journeys. The rain, the wind and the high surf are coming either way, and the only real question is how close to the coast the centre stays.

    Saturday: Heavy Rain and Strong Winds From Florida to the Carolinas

    The first heavy rain arrives on Saturday, stretching from Jacksonville in Florida up to Myrtle Beach in South Carolina as the storm strengthens offshore. By Saturday afternoon and into the overnight hours, the focus shifts to Georgia and the Carolinas, where the heaviest downpours are expected. Wind gusts of around 40 miles per hour or higher will whip the coast, driving rough surf onto beaches and raising the risk of coastal erosion — a serious concern for low-lying barrier islands such as the Outer Banks, where the shoreline can be eaten away in a single storm.

    Flood forecasters have placed a Level 2 out of 4 flash flood threat over the coast from northeastern Florida into coastal South Carolina for Saturday. A Level 2 threat means flash flooding is possible in many places, and the danger is higher because the ground in parts of Florida is already saturated after days of relentless rain. Drivers should expect flooded roads, and anyone living near the coast should be ready for water rising quickly with little warning.

    Sunday Into Monday: The Storm Travels North

    From there the storm is expected to travel up the coast. Forecasters now expect the centre of the coastal low to push closer to the Outer Banks sometime on Sunday, likely skimming the shoreline rather than coming fully ashore. As moisture surges northward, the risk of flash flooding spreads into the Carolinas and the Mid-Atlantic, and rain is expected to reach New England by Sunday.

    A Level 1 flash flood risk covers the coast from Norfolk in Virginia all the way into southeastern Maine on Sunday. The heaviest rain is forecast from Long Island through eastern Massachusetts and Maine, with a widespread one to two inches expected and localised pockets of three to five inches where the rain bands stall. In New England the rain will fall on cold ground, so ponding on roads and poor drainage flooding are the main concerns rather than river flooding.

    The exact timing and strength of the northern impacts are still uncertain, and small shifts in the track will make a big difference: a track fifty miles further east would spare the coast the worst of the rain, while a track fifty miles further west would bring the heaviest weather inland. That uncertainty is normal two days out, and it is why the forecast will keep changing through the weekend.

    Thanksgiving Travel Faces Disruption

    For travellers, the timing could hardly be worse. The weekend before Thanksgiving is one of the busiest travel periods of the year in the United States, and this storm threatens to snarl journeys across the Northeast on Monday, depending on how close it tracks to the coast. Flights into and out of airports from Washington to Boston could face delays, and drivers on the I-95 corridor should expect slow traffic, standing water and reduced visibility.

    The disruption is not limited to the East Coast. The same unsettled pattern is bringing storms to the West Coast too, so Thanksgiving travel weather is difficult on both sides of the country this year. If you are travelling, check the forecast on the morning you leave rather than the night before, because the storm's track is still being refined. Leave extra time, keep your phone charged for airline and traffic alerts, and have a backup route in mind if your usual road floods. Avoid coastal roads during high tide, when storm surge and high surf can push water across the highway with no warning.

    Florida's Flooding Is Still Going On

    The storm now heading up the coast is the same system that has been drowning Florida for days. Before sliding offshore, the low pressure centre sat over the state and hammered the Daytona Beach area with more torrential rain. Florida has endured days of relentless downpours that have flooded neighbourhoods from Brevard County to Broward County, prompted water rescues, closed schools and led the state to activate the National Guard.

    That flooding has not ended just because the storm is moving on. Rivers such as the St. Johns near Cocoa are at record levels, and water drains slowly from low-lying neighbourhoods. If you are in Florida, keep following local guidance and check the live forecast on the Miami weather page and the Orlando weather page. You can read our full report on the Florida flooding on the Florida Atlantic Coast flash flooding page.

    California: Flooded Streets in Los Angeles and San Francisco

    While the East Coast watches Kyle, the West Coast is dealing with its own powerful storms. In Los Angeles, severe weather has flooded streets across the city, leaving drivers stranded in rising water. In San Francisco, whole streets turned into rivers as heavy rain overwhelmed drains, trapping vehicles in floodwater and forcing water rescues. The same storm system has also brought mudslides to hillsides across the region, a familiar danger in California when intense rain falls on ground scarred by wildfire.

    California's storms are part of the same energetic pattern that has dominated American weather this week: a strong jet stream pulling storm after storm onshore in the west while a coastal low brews in the east. For the latest conditions, check the Los Angeles weather page and the San Francisco weather page, and never drive through a flooded road — the water is often deeper and faster than it looks.

    Utah: Winter Alerts as Snow Levels Fall

    Inland, winter is making its presence felt. Winter weather alerts are in effect across parts of Utah, where a soggy, cool pattern has kept moisture streaming across the state and snow levels are falling. Mountain roads may turn slick as the snow line drops, and drivers heading into the Wasatch Range should carry chains or use winter tyres and check road conditions before setting out. The change to colder air is a reminder that Thanksgiving week often marks the first real taste of winter across the interior West.

    How to Stay Safe in a Coastal Storm

    Coastal storms cause most of their harm through water, not wind. Flash flooding kills more people in the United States than any other weather hazard except heat, and most of those deaths happen in vehicles. Never drive through flood water, even if it looks shallow: it can hide a washed-out road or a strong current, and it takes surprisingly little moving water to sweep a car away. If you meet a flooded road, turn around and find another way.

    On the coast, stay off beaches, piers and rock walls while the surf is high. Large waves can sweep across a beach with no warning during a coastal storm, and rip currents become much stronger. Secure garden furniture, bins and anything else that can blow around before the gusts arrive, and keep torches and phone chargers ready in case of power cuts. If your area is under a flood warning, move valuables and documents to higher floors, and never wait to evacuate if officials tell you to leave.

    What Happens Next

    The next two days will decide how bad this storm becomes. Hurricane-hunter data, fresh model runs and the storm's own behaviour will narrow down the track, and the forecast for Sunday and Monday will sharpen through the weekend. The key things to watch are whether the centre stays offshore or clips the Outer Banks, and whether the heaviest rain bands set up over the big cities of the Northeast.

    Check the live forecast every morning this weekend. The New York weather page, the Boston weather page, the Philadelphia weather page, the Washington DC weather page and the Charlotte weather page all show hourly breakdowns, so you can see exactly when the rain and wind are expected where you live.     Frequently Asked Questions

    What is Subtropical Storm Kyle?

    Kyle is the next name on the Atlantic storm list. A coastal low off the Southeast is being watched by the National Hurricane Center for tropical or subtropical development, and if it strengthens enough it will be named Subtropical Storm Kyle. Even without a name, it will bring heavy rain and strong winds to the East Coast.

    Which areas will get the heaviest rain?

    On Saturday the heaviest rain falls from Jacksonville, Florida, to Myrtle Beach, South Carolina, and then across Georgia and the Carolinas. On Sunday the rain spreads into the Mid-Atlantic and New England, with the heaviest totals from Long Island through eastern Massachusetts and Maine, where one to two inches is expected and some spots could see three to five inches.

    Will the storm affect Thanksgiving travel?

    Yes, possibly. The storm could snarl travel in the Northeast on Monday, depending on how close it tracks to the coast. Flights and road journeys from Washington to Boston may face delays, and drivers should check conditions before leaving and allow extra time.

    Has a storm like this happened this late in November before?

    No named storm has formed in this area and tracked up the coast this late in November before, which makes Kyle unusual. Late-season coastal storms are not unheard of, but one with tropical development potential at this point in the year is rare.

    Is California also facing flooding?

    Yes. Powerful storms have flooded streets in Los Angeles and turned streets into rivers in San Francisco, trapping vehicles and forcing water rescues. Mudslides have also been reported. The West Coast storms are part of the same unsettled weather pattern affecting the whole country this week.

    Where can I check the live forecast for New York and Boston?

    Check the live forecast on the New York weather page and the Boston weather page on Weather Tomorrow. Both show hourly breakdowns with rain timing, wind and temperature, updated every hour from fresh model data.

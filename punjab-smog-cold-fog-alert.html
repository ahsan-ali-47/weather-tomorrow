    Punjab Smog Alert as Cold and Fog Close In

    Smog alert for 17 Punjab districts, dry cold weather across Pakistan, and fog on the motorways — what the forecasts say and how to stay safe.

    Lahore, Multan and Faisalabad face very unhealthy air, with no rain in sight to clear it.

    By Ahsan Ali, Founder, Weather Tomorrow · 22 November 2026

    Smog Alert Issued for 17 Punjab Districts

    Punjab is facing a difficult weekend of thick smog, cold nights and morning fog, and the official warning now covers much of the province. The National Emergency Operations Center of the National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA) issued an alert this week warning that smog would intensify over the next two days across several Punjab districts. The alert named Lahore, Kasur, Gujranwala, Hafizabad, Nankana Sahib, Sargodha, Sheikhupura, Sialkot, Narowal, Okara and Faisalabad, along with Jhang, Khanewal, Multan, Khanpur, Kot Addu and Bahawalpur and other parts of the province — 17 districts in total.

    The warning was not only about dirty air. The NDMA cautioned that worsening air quality could pose serious health risks, cut visibility, and disrupt road traffic, especially late at night and in the early morning when smog and fog combine. It advised children, older people and anyone with a breathing illness to avoid unnecessary time outdoors, and urged people to follow official weather updates and the Pak NDMA Disaster Alert app for timely guidance. That combination — a province-wide alert, very high pollution readings, and a dry forecast with no rain to wash the air clean — is what makes this weekend different from an ordinary hazy day.

    If you live in one of the named districts, the practical meaning is simple: plan your days around the smog, not through it. Check the live forecast for your own city before you leave home — the Lahore weather, Multan weather, Faisalabad weather, Gujranwala weather and Sialkot weather pages all carry the current conditions, the hourly outlook and the 10-day forecast — and treat the early morning as the worst part of the day, not the safest.

    Smog in Lahore Today: What the Air Quality Readings Mean

    Smog readings in Lahore have stayed in the unhealthy range for days, and the highest reported figures this week were far worse than that. Reporting on 20 November put Lahore's average air pollution index above 400, with a reading of up to 600 recorded in Gulberg and 550 on Burki Road. The same report put a particulate reading of 641 in Multan and 290 in Sargodha, while Lahore's minimum temperature had fallen to 12 degrees Celsius. Those are reported monitor readings, not a single official city average, and they show how bad the air can get street by street rather than giving one number for the whole city.

    By the evening of 21 November, a separate reading put Lahore's Air Quality Index (AQI) at 172, in the "Unhealthy" category, with fine particulate matter (PM2.5) named as the main pollutant. On the morning of Sunday 22 November, conditions in Lahore were described as smoke, with an AQI of 268 — "Very Unhealthy" — and PM2.5 again the primary pollutant, with calm winds and humidity above 70 percent. For Sunday itself, Lahore was forecast to be sunny with a high of about 26 degrees and a low of 12 degrees, and almost no chance of rain. In other words, the sky may look brighter by the afternoon, but the air does not become clean just because the sun comes out.

    Model data for 21 November told the same story across the Punjab plains. PM2.5 concentrations were put at 144.4 micrograms per cubic metre in Gujranwala, 140.2 in Lahore, 135.5 in Faisalabad and 130.9 in Sargodha — all in the "very poor" band — while Karachi, by contrast, sat at 36.7, in the "moderate" band. That gap matters: this is a Punjab plains problem this weekend, driven by local conditions, not a country-wide haze. Check the AQI and smog page for the latest air quality picture, and read our longer guide in Smog in Lahore for the background on why the city gets smog every winter.

    Why Do Smog Readings Differ From One Report to Another?

    It is normal for smog numbers to disagree, and the disagreement does not mean any one reading is wrong. Different monitors sit in different places — a busy road, a housing society, a rooftop — and they measure at different times of day, when the smog itself is changing. Some reports quote an AQI, which is an index that bands air quality from good to hazardous; others quote PM2.5 directly, in micrograms per cubic metre, which is the concentration of the tiny particles that get deepest into the lungs. A single very high reading at one monitor at one hour is a warning about that place and that hour. What matters for your day is the trend: whether the air in your city is staying unhealthy through the morning, easing a little at midday, and thickening again in the evening. That is why checking your own city's page, rather than remembering one headline number, is the habit that helps most.

    Why Smog Gets Worse When the Weather Is Dry and Cold

    The weather pattern behind this weekend's smog is almost the worst possible combination for clean air. The Pakistan Meteorological Department (PMD) said continental air was prevailing over most of the country, with a shallow trough of a westerly wave over the north-west, expected to persist for about 24 hours. In plain terms, that means dry air, light winds and very little change — exactly the conditions that let pollution build up instead of blowing away.

    Cold nights make the problem worse. After sunset, the air close to the ground cools faster than the air above it, and the smoke, dust and vehicle exhaust released during the day get trapped in a shallow layer near the surface instead of rising and dispersing. This is why smog and fog feel heaviest late at night and early in the morning, and why the same day can look clearer around midday when the sun has warmed the ground and stirred the air a little. Add calm winds, and there is nothing to push the trapped air away. Add no rain, and there is nothing to wash the particles out of the sky. November in the Punjab plains often works this way, which is why the smog season returns every year at about this time — but an NDMA alert and readings above 400 mean this is not a normal hazy spell to ignore.

    The dry pattern also explains why the smog is not expected to lift on its own in the next day or two. The PMD forecast for Saturday and Sunday was mainly dry weather across most of the country, with cold conditions in the north and partly cloudy skies in the upper regions. Long-range outlooks put Pakistan's seven-day rainfall total at zero millimetres, with no rainy days expected in the next 14 days. Without a rain system or a strong wind change, the air has no way to clear itself, so the sensible plan is to manage exposure day by day rather than wait for a sudden improvement.

    Cold Weather in Northern Pakistan: Sub-Zero Nights

    While Punjab deals with smog, the north of the country is dealing with cold. The PMD said dry weather had prevailed across most parts of the country in the past 24 hours, with cold conditions in the upper regions, and forecast that cold conditions would continue, particularly during the night and early morning in northern and upper areas. The lowest minimum temperatures it reported were minus 6 degrees Celsius at Leh, minus 3 degrees at Skardu and minus 1 degree at Gupis — a reminder of how quickly winter arrives in the mountains once November is under way.

    Islamabad and the surrounding areas were forecast to see partly cloudy weather on Saturday with cold nights, followed by dry weather on Sunday, with cold mornings and nights. The capital's outlook for Sunday 22 November was smoky haze, with a high of about 24 degrees and a low of 12 degrees, and no rain expected. Skardu, by contrast, was forecast to reach only about 7 degrees in the day on 22 November, falling to about minus 4 degrees overnight, with a small chance of wintry precipitation around midday. If you are travelling north, the advice is the same as in Punjab but for a different reason: dress for a cold night even if the afternoon feels mild, and check the forecast the morning you travel. See the Islamabad weather, Rawalpindi weather, Peshawar weather and Skardu weather pages for the live outlook in each city.

    Fog on the Motorways This Morning

    Smog is only half of the visibility problem this weekend. The other half is fog, and it is already showing up on the motorways. A fog forecast for the morning of Sunday 22 November showed fog on the M-1 Peshawar to Islamabad section, with visibility down to about 380 metres near Swabi between roughly 6:30 and 10:00 in the morning, while the other motorway sections it tracks were forecast to stay clear. The same forecast put smog levels very high on the N-5 Lahore to Okara to Sahiwal stretch, with PM2.5 up to 270 micrograms per cubic metre in the next 24 hours — hazardous air on a road that many people travel every day.

    Fog forecasts are model guidance, not a closure notice: fog can form in one stretch and clear in the next, and conditions can change within an hour. Before any early-morning or late-night journey, check the National Highway and Motorway Police travel advisory or call 130, and be ready to delay a non-essential trip if visibility is poor. The risk is highest when smog and fog arrive together, because the haze never fully lifts during the day and the fog then thickens again after dark. A journey that looks fine at noon can become a slow, low-visibility drive by evening, so build extra time into any plan that involves the M-1, the M-2 or the N-5 this weekend.

    How to Stay Safe in Smog and Fog

    The NDMA's core advice is the right starting point: children, older people and anyone with asthma or another breathing illness should avoid unnecessary outdoor exposure while the alert is in force, and everyone should keep up with official updates. Beyond that, a few practical habits make a real difference. First, time your day around the air. Keep outdoor exercise, school runs on foot and long errands away from the early morning and late evening, when smog is thickest, and use the clearer midday hours for anything that must be done outside. If you exercise, do it indoors this weekend — running or cycling in very unhealthy air works against the benefit of the exercise.

    Second, protect your breathing. A well-fitting mask that seals around the nose and mouth helps far more than a loose cloth, which lets most fine particles through around the edges. Keep windows closed during the worst hours, especially in bedrooms overnight, and if you use an air purifier, run it in the room where you sleep. Keep asthma inhalers and regular medicines close at hand, and seek medical advice promptly if breathing becomes difficult, wheezing increases, or a cough will not settle — do not wait for the air to improve on its own.

    Third, drive as if the road is shorter than it looks. In fog and smog, slow down well before you think you need to, switch on low-beam headlights and fog lights, keep a much larger gap to the vehicle ahead, and avoid sudden braking and overtaking. Use the road markings and the vehicle ahead's lights as your guide, not your memory of the road. If visibility drops to a few car lengths, pull over somewhere safe, well clear of moving traffic, and wait for it to improve. And if the journey is not essential, the safest choice on a foggy night is not to start it at all.

    When Will the Smog Clear?

    There is no quick fix in the forecast. With no rain expected in Lahore over the next few days, a seven-day rainfall total of zero for Pakistan as a whole, and no rainy days in the 14-day outlook, the air has nothing coming to wash it clean. Smog in the Punjab plains usually eases when a stronger wind system moves through, or when a rain-bearing westerly wave arrives — neither is in the immediate forecast, and the shallow westerly trough already over the north-west is too weak to change the pattern. That does not mean the smog will stay at its worst every hour: midday sunshine and any light breeze can thin it for a few hours. But the pattern of thick mornings, hazy middays and thick evenings is likely to repeat until the weather pattern itself changes.

    The useful response is to check daily rather than guess. Look at your city's forecast each morning, watch the AQI trend on the AQI and smog page, and note whether the readings are rising or falling over two or three days rather than reacting to a single hour. If you live in Lahore, Multan, Faisalabad or Gujranwala, expect the alert conditions to persist into the new week and plan accordingly — and if a rain system does appear in the forecast, that will be the first real sign of relief. Until then, the smog is a health and travel issue to manage, not a single bad day to ride out.

    Punjab Smog: The Bottom Line

    This weekend brings three weather problems at once in Pakistan: an NDMA smog alert covering 17 Punjab districts, with very unhealthy air in Lahore, Multan and other plains cities; dry, cold weather with sub-zero nights in the north and no rain in the forecast to clear the air; and morning fog on the motorways, worst on the M-1 near Swabi. The smog is thickest late at night and early in the morning, the air is unlikely to clear quickly, and the people most at risk are children, older people and anyone with a breathing illness. Limit outdoor time at the worst hours, wear a well-fitting mask when you must go out, drive slowly in fog, and check your city's live forecast every morning — starting with Lahore, Multan, Faisalabad and Islamabad — before you decide what the day holds.

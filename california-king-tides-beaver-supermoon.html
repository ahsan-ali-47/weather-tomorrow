    California Braces for Historic King Tides as Beaver Supermoon Peaks

    Exceptionally high tides, a supermoon and powerful surf are combining along the California coast this Thanksgiving week.

    Which beaches and roads are at risk, how high the water will get — and how to stay safe around the high tides.

    By Ahsan Ali, Founder, Weather Tomorrow · 24 November 2026

    What Are King Tides — and Why This Week Is Different?

    California is heading into some of its highest tides of the year, and this time the sea is expected to go further than usual. Fox Weather reports record-breaking king tides returning to the California coast this week, with ocean levels of more than nine feet expected in some places and a much higher risk of coastal flooding and beach erosion. A king tide is not a storm and it is not a tsunami. It is a non-scientific term for an exceptionally high tide, the kind that arrives when the pull of the Moon and the Sun line up and push the sea higher than normal. Because it is driven by the Moon, it is predictable — but predictable does not mean harmless. Seawater can still reach roads, car parks and low-lying homes on a dry day, with no rain at all.

    What makes this week different is how many factors are stacking up at once. The full moon is a supermoon, sitting unusually close to Earth. Extra swell energy is running along the coast, sea levels are higher than usual, and powerful surf is forecast for the middle of the week. Each factor on its own would lift the water a little. Together, they lift it enough to matter: a foot or two of extra height is often the difference between a wet beach and a flooded road. That is why warnings cover so much of the coast at the same time, from the San Francisco Bay Area in the north to San Diego in the south, and why forecasters are treating this as a flooding event rather than a curiosity for photographers.

    King tides also deserve attention for a longer reason. They show, in plain sight, what higher sea levels do to a coastline. Places that flood only on the highest tides today will flood more often as the sea rises, and the same low roads and beachfront car parks that go under this week are the first to feel every future high tide. Watching where the water reaches this week — which paths close, which car parks flood, which drains back up — is useful information for anyone who lives, works or travels along the California coast. It is a preview, and it is worth taking seriously.

    The Beaver Supermoon: Why the Moon Matters This Week

    The full moon at the centre of this event is November's Beaver Moon. It reaches its peak at 9:53 a.m. Eastern Time on 24 November, according to Fox Weather, with the full moon visible on the nights of 23 and 24 November, and it is the first of three consecutive supermoons. A supermoon is a full moon that turns full when the Moon is at or near the closest point in its orbit to Earth. Closer means a stronger gravitational pull, and a stronger pull means higher high tides. The name Beaver Moon comes from long tradition, recorded in sources such as the Farmers' Almanac: November is when beavers finish building their lodges and storing food for winter, before the ground freezes.

    The physics is simple to picture. The Moon's gravity pulls the ocean towards it, creating a bulge of water on the side of Earth facing the Moon. At a full moon, the Sun lines up with the Moon and Earth, so the Sun's pull adds to the Moon's pull and the tides grow larger. These are the spring tides, which happen twice a month. When the full moon also happens near the Moon's closest approach, the bulge grows a little more — and that little more is exactly what turns an ordinary high tide into a king tide. The difference sounds small until it meets a low road: water that would normally stop at the edge of the beach can cross a path, a car park or a shoreline road instead.

    For anyone hoping to see the supermoon itself, the best times are just after sunset on 23 and 24 November, looking east as the Moon rises. It will look large and bright near the horizon, as full moons always do when they first appear. Enjoy it from a safe, dry viewpoint well away from the water — a hill, a high street or a window with a clear eastern view — rather than from a beach that may be flooding at the same high tide. The Moon is not dangerous. The water below it, this week, can be.

    San Francisco Bay: Warnings, Water Levels and Record Talk

    The San Francisco Bay Area faces the sharpest risk. Coastal Flood Warnings are in effect from the San Francisco Bay Area to San Pablo Bay through Thursday, as exceptionally high tides raise the risk of coastal flooding. Fox Weather puts the predicted high tide in San Francisco at about 7 feet, roughly 1.8 feet higher than the average high tide of about 5.2 feet, with the high tide closest to the full moon falling in the late morning. Reports of the forecast total water level at the San Francisco tide gauge go higher still: around 8.4 feet on Tuesday, 24 November, and around 8.6 feet on Wednesday, 25 November, approaching the historical record of 8.66 feet set in 1983, with water up to about 2.7 feet above normal and flooding up to about 2.8 feet above ground level in the lowest places.

    Two different numbers can look confusing, so it helps to separate them. The first number is the predicted tide: how high the Moon and Sun alone would lift the sea. The second is the total water level actually expected at the gauge, which adds everything else on top — extra swell, higher-than-usual sea levels and any storm surge pushing water towards the shore. When forecasters warn of near-record water levels, they are talking about that total. Either way, the practical result is the same for people on the ground: the worst flooding comes around the late-morning high tides, above all on Wednesday, and low-lying shoreline roads, waterfront paths, ferry areas and beach car parks are the places to watch. Check the live forecast on the San Francisco weather page before setting out.

    Reports also describe these as the highest king tides in the Bay Area in 18 years, according to warnings passed on by the U.S. Coast Guard. Whether or not the final gauge reading breaks the 1983 record, the message from officials is consistent: treat this as a real flood risk, not a photo opportunity. Move cars out of low-lying waterfront car parks before the high tide, avoid shoreline roads at the peak, and expect some routes to close at short notice. If a road is already under water, do not try to drive through it to see how deep it is.

    Los Angeles and San Diego: High Tides and Coastal Flood Advisories

    Further south, the picture is calmer but still worth respecting. Coastal Flood Advisories are in effect from just north of Los Angeles south through San Diego, beginning on Tuesday and continuing through Thursday. Fox Weather reports that the high tide closest to the Beaver Moon in San Diego will arrive at around 8:34 a.m. on 25 November, reaching approximately 7.5 feet, while the equivalent high tide in Los Angeles is expected to reach about 7.2 feet. In Southern California, reports put the tides a foot or two above the average high tide. That is less dramatic than the Bay Area forecast, but it is still enough to push waves over seawalls, flood beach car parks and close low coastal paths at the top of the tide.

    Southern California flooding from king tides tends to arrive quietly. There is no storm to watch on the radar and often no rain at all. Instead, the sea simply keeps rising a little further than usual, until water is standing where people normally walk or park. Beach erosion is part of the same process: powerful waves strip sand from the beach, leaving less dry sand to absorb the next set of waves, so each high tide reaches a little further inland. Harbour walkways, bike paths along the shore, beachfront steps and the lowest rows of beach car parks are the usual first places to go under. Flood warnings have also been reported for Orange and San Diego counties, so the risk is not limited to one famous beach.

    Timing is everything in the south. The same beach can be wide, dry and perfectly safe at low tide, and flooded and dangerous a few hours later at high tide. If you are heading to the coast, check the tide time first and plan around it, rather than arriving at the peak and hoping for the best. Check the live forecast on the Los Angeles weather page and the San Diego weather page, and if a car park or path is already wet at the waterline, park higher up and walk in — or come back at low tide.

    High Surf, Rip Currents and 20-Foot Waves

    The tides are only half of this week's coastal story. A powerful, long-period swell is also forecast to strike the West Coast in the middle of Thanksgiving week, and the National Weather Service has issued a High Surf Advisory for Wednesday, 25 November, covering coastal beaches from Monterey County through the San Francisco Bay Area. Breaking waves of 20 to 30 feet are forecast from Point Reyes to Big Sur, including San Francisco and Monterey Bay, with waves of 15 to 22 feet on the Central Coast and 6 to 10 feet at exposed west-facing beaches in Southern California. When that surf meets a king tide, there is less beach for the waves to break on, so the water runs further, hits seawalls harder and floods places that a normal swell would never reach.

    Rip currents are the hidden danger in this kind of surf. A rip current is a narrow, fast-moving channel of water flowing away from the shore, and it can carry even a strong swimmer out past the breaking waves in seconds. High surf and king tides both make rip currents stronger and less predictable, and the currents can appear on beaches that look calm between sets of waves. Sneaker waves add a second risk: a much larger wave can arrive without warning after a run of smaller ones, sweeping across sand that looked dry moments earlier. That is why officials urge people to stay well back from the waterline, stay off rocks, jetties and piers, and never turn their back on the sea during a high surf event.

    For surfers, this week will tempt the experienced and punish the careless. Big, powerful surf on a king tide breaks closer to seawalls, steps and rocks, with less room to get out of the water safely. Anyone going in should have local knowledge, go with others, and be honest about whether the conditions are beyond their ability. For everyone else, the safest view of the surf is from well above the beach, behind a barrier, at a distance where spray cannot reach. A wave that looks impressive from a cliff path can be deadly from the sand below it.

    Thanksgiving Travel: Roads, Beaches and Getting Around the Coast

    All of this arrives in one of the busiest travel weeks of the American year. Millions of people are on the move for Thanksgiving, and many of them will be driving along the coast, visiting family near the shore, or heading to the beach on a free afternoon. The worst conditions cluster around the late-morning high tides from Tuesday through Thursday, with Wednesday the peak day in the Bay Area, so the timing of a journey matters as much as its destination. A coastal road that is clear in the early morning can be flooded by late morning and clear again by the afternoon — and a driver who has not checked the tide can easily arrive at exactly the wrong hour.

    The practical steps are straightforward. Check the forecast and any flood warning on the morning you travel, allow extra time for diversions, and do not leave a car parked overnight in a low beach car park or on a shoreline road that floods at high tide. If you are staying near the coast, move the car to higher ground before the high tide rather than after the water arrives. Waterfront transport routes, including ferries and low-lying roads around the Bay, can also be affected, so check before leaving and have an inland route in mind. Where a beach visit can move to low tide, move it: the beach will be wider, the surf further away, and the walk back to the car park dry.

    There is also a knock-on effect worth remembering. Flooded coastal roads do not only affect the people who live beside them; they push traffic onto inland routes that are already busy with holiday travellers. A closure that looks small on a map can add a long delay to a journey inland. That is another reason to leave early, check conditions twice — once before leaving and once before the return leg — and keep fuel, water and a phone charger topped up. The forecast will keep being refined as the peak tides approach, so a check made on Monday may not hold for Wednesday.

    How to Stay Safe During King Tides and Coastal Flooding

    Floodwater is the most dangerous part of this week's weather, precisely because it can look shallow and harmless. Never drive or walk through a flooded road: the water can hide a washed-out surface, an open drain or a strong current, and a small amount of fast-moving water can sweep a vehicle or a person away. If you meet floodwater, turn around and find another route, and never drive around barriers or warning signs. At the coast, the same rule applies to waves: stay off beaches, piers, rock walls and seawalls while the surf is high, because a single large wave can sweep across a beach or a promenade without warning.

    At home or at a coastal business, prepare before the high tide rather than during it. Move cars, bins, garden furniture and anything that can float or blow away to higher ground. Move valuables, documents and electrical items upstairs or onto high shelves if your building is in a low-lying area. Clear drains and gutters where you safely can, charge phones and torches, and know where your fuse box and water shut-off are. If officials close a road or advise people to move, follow that advice promptly rather than waiting to see how high the water gets. Read our guide on the Weather Alerts page to understand what each warning means.

    Look out for other people, too. Older neighbours, visitors who do not know the local tides, and children drawn to the excitement of big waves are all more at risk this week. A short warning — that the beach floods at high tide, that the car park goes under, that the rocks are slippery and the waves are reaching the wall — can prevent a frightening situation. And if you see someone in difficulty in the water, do not go in after them: call emergency services, keep watching from a safe place, and throw a flotation aid or a rope if one is available without putting yourself in danger.

    What Happens Next: When the Tides Ease

    The peak of this event is short. The highest tides fall on Tuesday and Wednesday, 24 and 25 November, with warnings running through Thursday, Thanksgiving Day, and the surf easing after the middle of the week. As the Moon moves away from its closest point and the full moon passes, the tides will step down again and flooded roads and paths should reopen. But the effects will linger: beaches will have lost sand, dunes and cliffs may be undercut, and debris can be left on roads and promenades. A beach that looks normal again the day after a king tide may have a steeper drop-off, exposed rocks or a weaker dune than it had before, so take care on the first visits back.

    This supermoon is also only the first of three in a row. December and January will bring further supermoons, and with them further rounds of higher-than-usual tides. Not every one will combine with powerful surf and higher sea levels in the same way, but coastal residents would do well to note which places flooded this week and to plan for the next high-tide cycle before it arrives. For forecasters, this week is a useful test: where the water went, and where it did not, sharpens the picture of which parts of the California coast flood first.

    Check the live forecast every morning while the warnings are in place. The San Francisco weather page, the Los Angeles weather page and the San Diego weather page all show hourly breakdowns for the coast, so you can plan each day around the tide rather than guessing. Stay back from the surf, give the sea room at high tide, and enjoy the supermoon from somewhere dry and safe.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    What is a king tide?

    A king tide is an exceptionally high tide — a non-scientific term for the highest tides of the year. It happens when the Moon and Sun line up and their gravitational pull combines, and the effect is strongest when a full moon is also near its closest point to Earth. King tides are predictable and natural, but the water they bring is real: seawater can reach roads, car parks and low-lying homes even on a dry, sunny day.

    When is the Beaver Supermoon in November 2026?

    The Beaver Moon reaches its peak at 9:53 a.m. Eastern Time on 24 November 2026, with the full moon visible on the nights of 23 and 24 November. It is a supermoon, a full moon near the closest point in its orbit to Earth, and the first of three consecutive supermoons. The best views are just after sunset on 23 and 24 November, looking east, from a safe spot well away from flooded beaches and high surf.

    Which parts of California are under coastal flood warnings?

    Coastal Flood Warnings are in effect from the San Francisco Bay Area to San Pablo Bay through Thursday, and Coastal Flood Advisories run from just north of Los Angeles south through San Diego from Tuesday through Thursday. Low-lying shoreline roads, beach car parks, harbour walkways and waterfront paths are the places most likely to flood, especially around the late-morning high tides.

    How high will the water get in San Francisco?

    The predicted high tide in San Francisco is about 7 feet, roughly 1.8 feet above the average high tide of about 5.2 feet. Total water levels at the tide gauge are forecast higher once extra swell and higher-than-usual sea levels are added, approaching the 1983 record of 8.66 feet, with up to about 2.7 feet above normal and flooding up to about 2.8 feet above ground in the lowest spots. The two numbers describe different things: the tide itself, and the total water level on the day.

    Is it safe to go to the beach during king tides?

    Not at high tide, and not while high surf warnings are in effect. King tides shrink the dry beach, waves can run much further up the sand than usual, and rip currents are stronger. Keep well back from the water, stay off rocks, piers and seawalls, never turn your back on the sea, and keep children and pets away from the waterline. Check the forecast before you go and visit at low tide instead.

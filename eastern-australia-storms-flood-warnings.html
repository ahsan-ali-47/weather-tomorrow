    Storms and Flood Warnings Hit Eastern Australia

    Heavy rain and storms move across Queensland, New South Wales, Victoria and Tasmania on the last weekend of spring.

    How much rain fell, which rivers have flood warnings, and how to stay safe in heavy rain.

    By Ahsan Ali, Founder, Weather Tomorrow · 28 November 2026

    Storms and Flood Warnings in Eastern Australia: What Happened

    Heavy rain and strong storms hit eastern Australia on the last weekend of spring, Saturday 28 November 2026. A band of rain and storms moved from central Queensland through New South Wales to Victoria and Tasmania. The Bureau of Meteorology, the official weather office in Australia, put severe weather warnings and flood watches in place across the eastern states. A flood watch means floods may happen soon in an area, so people there should get ready.

    The rain came from warm, wet air from the tropics. The tropics are the hot parts of the world near the equator. That wet air met a dip in the upper air, and together they made rain and storms over a very large area. News reports call this spring the wettest spring on record in an El Niño year. El Niño is a warm water pattern in the Pacific Ocean. It can change how much rain falls in Australia. This spring had a lot of rain in the south-east, but less rain earlier in winter. That mix helped make this weekend so wet.

    This post explains what happened, how much rain fell, which areas have flood warnings, and how to stay safe. All the facts here come from news reports and official warnings on 28 November 2026. Check the live forecast before you travel, because rain and warnings can change fast.

    How Much Rain Fell in Eastern Australia?

    A lot of rain fell in a short time. In Victoria, the heaviest rain overnight fell at Amphitheatre Reservoir, in the northern Pyrenees area. It got 85.4 mm of rain. Bet Bet Creek, in the west of Victoria, got 72.8 mm. For scale, 85 mm of rain is about 85 litres of water on every square metre of ground. That is a lot of water in one night, and it runs off the land fast.

    The rain did not stop in the morning. The north-east and eastern mountain areas of Victoria were set to get 55 to 70 mm more rain in six hours on Saturday morning. Six hours is a short time for that much rain. When rain falls that fast, small rivers and creeks rise fast too, and roads can flood with little warning.

    Friday gave an early sign of what was coming. Weather reports say a storm dropped 58 mm of rain in one hour at Warracknabeal, in Victoria, around midday on Friday. One hour of rain like that can flood streets and low land in a town very fast. It also shows why the warnings came early. The system was already strong before the weekend started.

    Rain totals like these do not tell the full story on their own. How fast the rain falls matters as much as how much falls in total. Slow, steady rain soaks into dry ground. Fast, heavy rain runs off the ground and fills drains, creeks and rivers. That is why flash flooding is a risk in this kind of storm. Flash flooding means water rises very fast, often in less than six hours, and it can catch people by surprise.

    Which Areas Have Flood Warnings in Australia?

    On Saturday 28 November 2026, the Bureau of Meteorology had flood warnings and flood watches in place in four states. In Queensland, a moderate flood warning was in place for Warrill Creek. Flood warnings were also in place for other rivers in Queensland, and a flood watch covered parts of southern and south-east Queensland. A flood watch covers a wide area where floods may happen, while a flood warning names a river where floods are expected or are already happening.

    In Victoria, flood watches covered most of the river areas in the west, centre and north-east of the state. A moderate flood warning was in place for the Loddon River, and other rivers in the west of the state also had warnings. In Tasmania, first flood warnings were in place for the Meander and Mersey Rivers, and a flood watch covered parts of the north-west, centre and north-east. In New South Wales, a flood watch covered the Upper Murray and Upper Macintyre river areas, where minor flooding is possible from Sunday.

    Rivers do not rise at the same time as the rain falls. Rain in the hills can take hours, or even a day, to reach towns downstream. Downstream means further along the river, where the water flows next. So flood risk can stay high even after the rain stops and the sky clears. If you live near a river or a creek, keep checking warnings after the rain ends.

    What Is a Flood Watch?

    A flood watch is an early warning. It means floods may happen in the area in the next day or two, because heavy rain is expected or has already fallen. It does not mean floods have started. It means: get ready, check warnings often, and do not plan travel through low areas without checking first. If a flood warning follows, it means floods are expected or are happening in a named river area, and people there should act on local advice.

    What Is the Difference Between Minor and Moderate Flooding?

    In general, minor flooding means water covers low land, parks and some roads. It can make driving hard, but most homes stay dry. Moderate flooding is more serious. More water covers more land, some homes and farms may be at risk, and some roads may close. The words minor and moderate are part of the official warning, so always read the full warning for your area. It will say which roads and towns are at risk.

    Strong Wind and Storms: What Else Is Coming?

    Rain is not the only risk this weekend. Strong, damaging wind was forecast for the mountain areas of New South Wales and for the central and eastern parts of Victoria. Damaging wind can bring down trees and power lines, and it can damage roofs. If you hear a severe weather warning for wind in your area, move cars away from large trees where you can, and stay inside during the worst gusts.

    Severe storms were also forecast for inland Queensland and northern New South Wales. A severe storm is a storm that can bring heavy rain, strong wind and large hail. Hail is small balls of ice that fall from storm clouds. Even small hail can damage cars and crops. If a severe storm is near you, stay inside and away from windows.

    The system was set to move east through Saturday evening and night, and reach the New South Wales coast by night. Storms were still likely along the Queensland coast on Sunday. That eastward move matters for people on the coast. A dry morning on the coast does not mean the day will stay dry. Check the forecast for your city before you make weekend plans, and check again in the afternoon.

    If you are in Sydney, check the live forecast on the Sydney weather page before you go out. If you are in Melbourne, check the Melbourne weather page. Both pages show the 10-day outlook and the hourly forecast, so you can see which hours are wet and which hours are dry.

    How Did the Storm Affect People in Victoria?

    The storm kept emergency workers busy in Victoria. The State Emergency Service, or SES, is the group that helps people in floods and storms in Australia. In Victoria, SES volunteers took more than 340 calls for help in 24 hours, up to 6 a.m. on Saturday. Most of the calls were about fallen trees (105 calls), damage to buildings (96 calls) and flooding (97 calls). The rest were for other storm help.

    Those numbers show how a storm like this hits daily life. Fallen trees block roads and paths. Building damage can mean a roof leak or a fence down, and it can also mean a home that is not safe to stay in until it is checked. Flooding can close roads and cut off short trips that people make every day. Even when no one is hurt, a storm like this costs time, money and sleep.

    Farms also feel this kind of rain. Heavy rain and storms can damage crops and delay the harvest. The harvest is the time when farmers gather ripe crops from the fields. Wet fields are hard for farm machines to use, and ripe crops can spoil if they stay wet. For people in towns, the main effect is simpler: wet roads, delayed trips and weekend plans that change at the last minute.

    Heat in the West While the East Gets Rain

    Australia is so large that two very different kinds of weather can happen on the same day. While the east got rain and storms, the west got heat. A heatwave in the Northern Territory was getting weaker, but in Western Australia it was getting stronger, from severe to extreme, in the Kimberley and Pilbara areas. A heatwave is a run of very hot days and nights.

    Kununurra, in the Kimberley, was set for daytime highs of 42 or 43 degrees for the next few days, with nights only falling to about 27 degrees. Nights that stay near 27 degrees give little rest from the heat, and that is hard for old people, young children and people who work outside. A low pressure area off the west coast was also set to bring a short, low-level heatwave to just north of Perth.

    The contrast is a good reminder to check the weather for the exact place you are in. News about rain in the east says nothing about heat in the west, and the other way round. Always check your own city, not just the national news.

    How to Stay Safe in Heavy Rain and Floods

    The most important safety rule in heavy rain is simple: never drive through floodwater. Water that looks shallow can hide a road that has washed away. Fast water can carry a car away, even when the water is not deep. Turn around and find another way, even if it takes longer. Never drive around road blocks or signs that close a flooded road. The signs are there because the road is not safe.

    Before you travel, check the official warnings for your area on the Bureau of Meteorology website or app, and check the live forecast for your city. Allow extra time for every trip, because roads may be slow, closed or under water. Keep your phone charged so you can check warnings and call for help. Tell someone where you are going and when you expect to arrive if you must drive in bad weather.

    If you need help in a flood or storm in Australia and it is not a danger to life, call the SES on 132 500. If life is in danger, call triple zero (000). Stay away from rivers, creeks and drains in heavy rain, because water levels can rise fast. Do not walk through floodwater either. It can be deeper than it looks, and it can hide drains, holes and live power lines.

    At home, clear drains and gutters if it is safe to do so, and move cars and valuables to higher ground if floodwater is near. If you are told to leave your home by emergency workers, leave early and do not wait for the water to arrive. After the storm, stay away from fallen power lines and damaged trees, and report them to the right local service.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    Why is eastern Australia getting so much rain?

    Warm, wet air from the tropics is being swept into the eastern states, and it met a dip in the upper air. Together they made a very active rain and storm system. It comes at the end of a spring that news reports call the wettest spring on record in an El Niño year. El Niño is a warm water pattern in the Pacific Ocean that can change how much rain falls in Australia.

    Which states have flood warnings?

    On Saturday 28 November 2026, flood warnings and flood watches were in place in Queensland, New South Wales, Victoria and Tasmania. Queensland had a moderate flood warning for Warrill Creek. Victoria had flood watches over most river areas in the west, centre and north-east, and Tasmania had first flood warnings for the Meander and Mersey Rivers. Warnings change fast, so check the Bureau of Meteorology for the latest warning in your area.

    How much rain fell in Victoria?

    The heaviest rain overnight in Victoria was 85.4 mm at Amphitheatre Reservoir, in the northern Pyrenees area, and 72.8 mm at Bet Bet Creek in the west. The north-east and eastern mountain areas were set to get 55 to 70 mm more rain in six hours on Saturday morning. On Friday, a storm dropped 58 mm of rain in one hour at Warracknabeal.

    Is it safe to drive in heavy rain?

    Only if you must, and with great care. Slow down, keep a long gap to the car in front, and use your headlights. Never drive through floodwater, and never drive around road blocks on a closed road. If you cannot see the road well in heavy rain, pull over somewhere safe and wait. Check warnings before you leave, and allow extra time.

Kununurra was set for 42 or 43 degrees in the day and about 27 degrees at night. Always check the weather for your own city.

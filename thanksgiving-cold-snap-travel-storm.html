    Thanksgiving Cold Snap and Coastal Storm Hit Holiday Travel

    A warm weekend ends in a sharp temperature drop for 200 million Americans, just as a powerful storm lines up the East Coast.

    Where the cold hits first, when the rain and wind peak — and how to plan a Thanksgiving journey around both.

    By Ahsan Ali, Founder, Weather Tomorrow · 24 November 2026

    A Warm Weekend, Then the Bottom Drops Out

    Thanksgiving week in the United States is starting with a weather whiplash. Much of the eastern half of the country spent the weekend 10 to 20 degrees warmer than average, with parts of the Interstate 95 corridor climbing into the 60s and some places reaching the 70s. That warmth does not last. A sweeping cold front is now breaking it down across the Midwest, letting cooler Canadian air spread south and east, and by Wednesday more than 200 million Americans could be living with below-average temperatures. It is a drastic reversal in just a few days, and it lands on the busiest travel week of the American year.

    The change will be easy to feel even though it is not, for most eastern cities, a deep freeze. Many areas that enjoyed the 60s and 70s over the weekend could find themselves back in the 40s and 50s by midweek, with colder conditions further north. Highs across the Midwest could stay mainly in the 30s and 40s. That is the kind of drop that catches people out: a jacket that felt unnecessary on Sunday is suddenly not enough on Wednesday, cars need longer to warm up, and anyone travelling with older relatives or small children should plan for real cold at the destination, not the mild weather they left behind. Check the live forecast on the Chicago weather page and the Detroit weather page before packing, because the Midwest feels this front first.

    Behind the front, the jet stream is dipping further south across the Central and Eastern United States, and that dip is what opens the door for the colder air to keep spreading. This is not one cold morning that warms up by lunchtime. The colder pattern moves east through midweek and settles in, so the Thanksgiving table itself is likely to be set in noticeably colder air than the weekend that preceded it. Anyone who has already travelled should expect the return journey to feel colder still, especially at night, when clear skies behind the front let temperatures fall further.

    The East Coast Storm: Rain, Wind and Coastal Flooding

    The cold snap is only half of the story. At the same time, a powerful coastal storm is developing along the East Coast, and the National Weather Service's Weather Prediction Center says confidence is high that it will bring heavy rain, high winds and coastal impacts on Wednesday and Thanksgiving Day. A coastal low is expected to spread heavy rain and strong winds along the Southeast and Mid-Atlantic coasts on Wednesday, then intensify and lift north on Thanksgiving itself, carrying heavy rain and damaging winds into the Mid-Atlantic and the Northeast, including the I-95 corridor. The heavy rain threat is greatest on Thanksgiving Day along the Mid-Atlantic and Northeast coasts, where an unusually moist flow of air is expected to stream into the region.

    The wind is a serious part of this storm, not an afterthought. Gusts of more than 50 mph are forecast along the coast, with gusts of 40 mph or more inland into the I-95 corridor, and in the latest discussion gusts of 45 mph or more inland as the storm moves north. Power cuts and tree damage are possible, and the tight pressure gradient keeps the wind going into Friday night. Strong onshore winds, high surf and high astronomical tides could also bring significant coastal flooding and beach erosion from Georgia up through the Northeast, with the flooding risk lingering along the immediate coast through Saturday. Anyone living or staying near the shore should take coastal flood warnings seriously this week, and should not assume a road that was dry in the morning will stay dry through the high tide.

    There is also a wintry side to the system, arriving after the rain. Snow showers are possible on the back side of the storm on Friday into Saturday, mainly over the Central Appalachians, the Lower Great Lakes and the interior Northeast and New England, with minor impacts likely. That means the return leg of a Thanksgiving trip could look very different from the outward leg: rain and wind going, then cold, wind and snow showers coming back, on roads that may still be wet. Conditions in the East are expected to improve by Sunday, but Friday and Saturday are not days to assume an easy drive home. Check the live forecast on the New York weather page, the Boston weather page and the Washington DC weather page on the morning of travel, and again before the return.

    Why This Week Is So Busy — and So Exposed

    Thanksgiving is the one week when a forecast like this affects the most people at once. Tens of millions of Americans travel for the holiday, many of them by road along the same I-95 corridor this storm is aimed at, and many more by air through the big Northeast and Mid-Atlantic airports. Rain and strong winds do not have to close an airport to cause trouble: they slow departures, stack up delays, and a delay that starts in New York or Philadelphia in the morning can still be affecting flights in Chicago or Atlanta by the evening. The same is true on the roads, where one flooded stretch or one fallen tree can push a long line of holiday traffic onto a diversion that was already busy.

    The timing makes it worse. The storm's worst coastal weather falls on Wednesday and Thanksgiving Day, exactly when the most people are on the move, and the cold snap arrives with it, so travellers face wet, windy and cold conditions together rather than one at a time. Add the California coast to the same week — king tides and high surf are already threatening coastal flooding there, as covered in our California king tides post — and the picture is of a country where both coasts need watching at once. The sensible response is not to cancel plans, but to build slack into them: an earlier departure, a route with an inland alternative, and a willingness to change the travel day if the forecast for your own city turns against you.

    There is one more knock-on effect worth remembering. Flooded coastal roads and closed routes do not only affect the people who live beside them. They push traffic inland, onto roads that are already carrying holiday travellers, and a closure that looks small on a map can add a long delay to a journey that never goes near the sea. The same applies to the return: Friday's wind and snow showers in the interior Northeast can slow the roads home even after the coastal rain has eased. Leave early, keep fuel and phone charge topped up, and check conditions twice — once before leaving and once before the return leg — because a forecast made on Monday may not hold for Thursday.

    How to Plan Around the Cold and the Storm

    Start with the forecast for the exact city you are travelling to, not the national picture. The cold arrives first in the Midwest and spreads east, while the storm arrives first in the Southeast and moves north, so two travellers heading in opposite directions can face completely different weather on the same day. Check the hourly breakdown for rain timing, wind and temperature on the city's Weather Tomorrow page — the Philadelphia weather page, the Atlanta weather page and the Miami weather page all show the same hourly detail — and plan the departure around the worst hours rather than through them.

    Pack for the destination's Wednesday weather, not the weekend's. Layers matter more than one heavy coat when temperatures swing 20 degrees in a few days: a warm layer, a windproof outer layer and something waterproof will cover most of what this week can throw at a traveller. At home, the same cold snap is a reminder to protect outdoor plants, cover exposed pipes where they are at risk, and bring pets inside overnight as temperatures fall. Drivers should allow extra time, keep a full tank, and never drive through a flooded road — floodwater can hide a washed-out surface or a strong current, and a small amount of fast-moving water can sweep a vehicle away. If a road is under water, turn around and find another route.

    Finally, watch the warnings, not just the forecast numbers. A Coastal Flood Warning, a High Wind Warning or a Winter Weather Advisory each means something specific, and our Weather Alerts page explains what each warning means and what to do about it. If officials close a road or advise people to move cars out of a low-lying area, follow that advice promptly rather than waiting to see how high the water gets. The storm will pass — conditions in the East improve by Sunday — but the travellers who fare best this week will be the ones who planned for the forecast they were actually given, checked it again on the morning, and left themselves room to change their minds.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    How cold will it get for Thanksgiving?

    By Wednesday 25 November, more than 200 million Americans are expected to see below-average temperatures. Places that were in the 60s and 70s over the weekend could fall back into the 40s and 50s, with highs mainly in the 30s and 40s across the Midwest. Temperatures are not generally expected to reach freezing in the eastern cities, but the change will feel sharp after such a warm weekend.

    When is the East Coast storm worst?

    The storm is expected to affect the East Coast from Wednesday through Saturday, with the heaviest rain along the Mid-Atlantic and Northeast coasts on Thanksgiving Day itself. Strong winds linger into Friday, snow showers follow on the back side of the system on Friday into Saturday, and conditions in the East improve by Sunday.

    How strong will the winds be?

    Gusts of more than 50 mph are forecast along the coast, with gusts of 40 mph or more inland into the I-95 corridor. Power cuts and tree damage are possible, and strong onshore winds, high surf and high tides could bring significant coastal flooding and beach erosion from Georgia up through the Northeast.

    Will it snow for Thanksgiving?

    Some snow is possible on the back side of the storm, mainly over the Central Appalachians, the Lower Great Lakes and the interior Northeast and New England on Friday into Saturday, with minor impacts likely. The main travel threat on Thanksgiving Day itself is heavy rain and wind along the coast, not snow.

    Where can I check the live forecast for my city?

    Check the live forecast on the New York weather page, the Boston weather page, the Washington DC weather page, the Philadelphia weather page, the Chicago weather page and the Detroit weather page on Weather Tomorrow. Each shows hourly breakdowns with rain timing, wind and temperature, updated from fresh model data.

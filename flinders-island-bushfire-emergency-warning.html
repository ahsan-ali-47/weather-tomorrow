    Flinders Island Bushfire: Emergency Warning as Wind Drives Fire South

    A lightning-started fire has burned about 3,000 hectares on a remote Tasmanian island, and a wind change has pushed it south toward homes.

    What started the fire, why Wednesday turned dangerous — and when rain may finally help.

    By Ahsan Ali, Founder, Weather Tomorrow · 25 November 2026

    Flinders Island Bushfire Today: Emergency Warnings in Place

    Flinders Island woke on Wednesday to a bushfire emergency. The island sits in Bass Strait, off the north-east coast of Tasmania, and it is a small, remote community: about 130,000 hectares in size, home to around 900 people, and known for farming exports including beef, lamb, wool and seafood. That remoteness is part of what makes this fire so serious. When a fire turns dangerous here, there are few roads out, help has further to travel, and the people facing the fire are often the same people who know the land best. On Wednesday morning, fire authorities issued emergency warnings for several settlements, and some residents were told it was already too late to leave.

    The warnings cover the places the fire is moving toward. Emergency warnings were in place for the coastal settlement of Killiecrankie and the nearby areas of Leeka and Mount Tanner, where residents were warned that an uncontrolled fire travelling south-west could put lives and homes in danger. A separate emergency warning told people at Mount Boyes, Carnacs Flat and Lughrata to leave immediately. Watch and Act warnings — meaning conditions are changing and people should act now — covered areas north of Killiecrankie Road, Mount Killiecrankie and Mount Blyth, and Palana, while advice-level warnings covered Palana Road, Wingaroo, Emita, Wybalenna and surrounding areas. Tasmania Police were helping to evacuate people, particularly around Leeka and Mount Tanner, and Killiecrankie Beach was named as a place of last resort for anyone who could no longer leave safely.

    Some structures had already been destroyed, but on Wednesday it was still too early to tell whether any of them were homes, according to Tasmania Fire Service incident controller Steven Richardson. Nearly 40 firefighters were working on the fire, along with 12 parks and wildlife staff, five helicopters and three aircraft, and additional resources were being sent in. The fire service also warned that the fire was threatening evacuation routes, that leaving once the fire arrived could put lives at risk, and that anyone who had already left should not return. In a community of this size, that advice is not abstract: the road you would normally take out may be the same road the smoke has already closed.

    How Lightning and a Wind Change Turned a Small Fire Into an Emergency

    This fire did not start in a town, and it did not start on Wednesday. It was started by a lightning strike last week, in the north of the island near Palana, and it burned mostly through reserved land — coastal scrub and forest, with organic soils underneath. That kind of ground matters. Organic soils can burn deep below the surface, and a fire burning in them is often difficult to put out completely, even when the flames on top look under control. For days, the fire was a known problem being worked on. Then the weather changed, and the problem changed with it.

    The first break came on Monday 23 November, when a very strong westerly cold front crossed Tasmania and the fire broke through its containment lines. Containment lines are the cleared edges and control lines crews build around a fire to stop it spreading, and losing them means the fire is no longer held in one place. By Tuesday, about 3,000 hectares had burned. Fire authorities put the size at 3,000 to 4,000 hectares by Wednesday, after the fire spread further south. To put that in proportion: 3,000 hectares is only a small share of an island of 130,000 hectares, but a fire does not need to burn most of an island to threaten the places where people actually live, especially when those places sit in its path.

    Wednesday was the dangerous day because of the wind. A wind change was forecast for Wednesday, and authorities feared it would push the fire further — and that is what happened, with the fire spreading south after the change. Northerly winds had already been pushing the smoke south across Bass Strait. As the wind picked up, fire crews found the conditions harder and harder to work in. Parks and Wildlife deputy incident controller Chris Emms said the winds were certainly picking up and pushing the fire's intensity, making a direct attack unsafe at that point. Incident controller Steven Richardson described the conditions as challenging, and pointed to the vegetation itself: flammable coastal scrub mixed with low forest trees had helped the fire spread. When wind, dry scrub and a change in direction arrive together, a fire can grow sideways as well as forwards, and the edge that was safe in the morning can become the front by the afternoon. That is why warnings can change quickly, and why the official warning level for a place can jump from advice to emergency in a single update.

    Smoke Across Bass Strait: Why the Air Turned Hazy Hundreds of Kilometres Away

    The most visible sign of this fire is not on Flinders Island at all. It is the smoke. Australia's Bureau of Meteorology reported on Wednesday morning that smoke from the fire burning over northern Flinders Island was streaming south across north-east Tasmania, reaching as far as St Helens, as northerly winds pushed the plume across Bass Strait. An emergency warning was in place from 10:43am AEDT on Wednesday. Satellite imagery on Wednesday morning showed the plume clearly, and residents on the island described a smoke front moving down the length of the island itself. Farmer Mick Grimshaw, who helped friends move cattle to safety and lost some fences, told ABC radio that they had been under the ashes, that the animals were safe, and that the smoke front could be seen heading right down the guts of the island.

    Smoke that travels this far is more than an unpleasant view. On Flinders Island, several roads were closed because smoke had cut visibility, and the state's Parks Minister, Nick Duigan, said there was a lot of smoke in the area and that it was now coming down Tasmania's coast. Poor visibility is dangerous in itself: a road can look passable until a thicker band of smoke drops visibility to almost nothing, and a driver who enters smoke at speed has no safe way out. That is one reason road closures during a bushfire should be treated as safety decisions, not inconveniences. Smoke also affects breathing, especially for children, older people and anyone with asthma or another lung condition, and it can linger and thicken again when the wind shifts, even after a clearer spell.

    For readers following the wider Australian weather, the smoke is a reminder that a fire in one place changes the air in another. The Sydney weather page and the Melbourne weather page show the live forecast, wind and conditions for Australia's biggest cities, and the Auckland weather page covers the nearest major New Zealand city across the Tasman Sea. None of those pages carries a fire warning for Flinders Island itself — fire warnings come from the Tasmania Fire Service and TasALERT, and during an emergency those official sources should always come first. But checking the wind direction and forecast for the region helps explain why smoke behaves the way it does: wind does not respect the water between an island and the mainland, and a northerly wind will carry smoke south whether there is land in between or not.

    What Happens Next: Rain on the Way, but the Wind Comes First

    The outlook offers real hope, but not instant relief. A large band of rain and thunderstorms is expected to sweep across central, eastern and south-eastern Australia during the rest of this week, reaching part of every state and territory, and forecasters expect the rain to help reduce or extinguish the Flinders Island fire from Friday into Saturday. The fire was still at emergency warning level on Wednesday afternoon, so the rain cannot come too soon for the people on the island. At the same time, the same system brings its own dangers: large areas of eastern and south-eastern Australia face a risk of severe thunderstorms from Friday to Sunday, with storms possible during both day and night because of intense atmospheric instability, and flooding is possible in some areas. The Bureau of Meteorology is likely to issue warnings for flooding and severe thunderstorms as the system develops. In other words, the weather that helps the fire may hurt somewhere else, and anyone travelling or working outdoors in eastern Australia this weekend should watch the warnings, not just the rain totals.

    It is also worth remembering how early this is in the season. The Flinders Island fire is burning early in the summer fire season, started by lightning and driven by wind through dry coastal scrub. One wet weekend will help this fire, but it does not end the season, and it does not remove the conditions that let a lightning strike become an emergency: dry vegetation, strong wind and a sudden change in wind direction. A fire that has burned deep into organic soils needs thorough checking and extinguishing before it can be called safe, and a forecast can still change between Wednesday and the weekend. The right way to read the rain forecast is as a turning point to hope for, not a promise to rely on. Until the rain arrives and the official warnings are downgraded, the fire remains an emergency, and the wind remains the factor that decides what happens hour by hour.

    How to Stay Safe Near Smoke and Bushfire

    If you are on Flinders Island or anywhere under a bushfire warning, follow the official warning for your exact area, and check it again often. Tasmania uses three warning levels. An Advice warning means a fire has started but there is no immediate danger, and people should stay informed. A Watch and Act warning means conditions are changing and people need to take action now, which may include leaving. An Emergency Warning is the highest level: people are in danger and must act immediately to survive. The Tasmania Fire Service and TasALERT carry the current warnings, maps and advice, and they should be checked before any decision to travel. Our Weather Alerts page explains what different weather warnings mean in general, but during a live fire, the fire service warning for your street or settlement is the one that counts.

    When a warning says it is too late to leave, do not try to drive out through the fire. Take shelter before the fire arrives, in a solid structure if one is available, and stay there until the danger passes. Anyone who has already left should not return until authorities say it is safe, because roads may be closed, visibility may be poor, and a fire can change direction with the wind. If you must drive in smoke, slow down, use headlights, and turn back if visibility falls — never drive into thick smoke hoping it will clear on the other side. In smoky air, stay indoors where possible, keep windows and doors closed, and avoid hard physical work outside. Anyone who has trouble breathing in smoke should seek medical help promptly. In a life-threatening emergency in Australia, call triple zero (000), and contact the State Emergency Service on 132 500 for storm and flood assistance. Keep your phone charged, keep fuel in the vehicle, and have a plan for where you would go and what you would take if the warning level for your area rises. The people of Flinders Island did not choose this week, and the wind did not ask their permission — but a plan made before the warning arrives is still the best protection any household has.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    What started the Flinders Island bushfire?

    A lightning strike last week started the fire in the north of Flinders Island. It was burning through coastal scrub and forest on reserved land, and it broke through its containment lines on Monday 23 November when a very strong westerly cold front crossed Tasmania.

    How big is the Flinders Island fire?

    About 3,000 hectares had burned by Tuesday 24 November, and fire authorities put the size at 3,000 to 4,000 hectares on Wednesday. Flinders Island covers about 130,000 hectares and is home to about 900 people. The fire has been spreading south, and emergency warnings cover Killiecrankie, Leeka and Mount Tanner.

    Why did the fire spread so fast on Wednesday?

    A wind change on Wednesday pushed the fire south after northerly winds had already carried its smoke across Bass Strait. Picking-up winds, flammable coastal scrub and low forest trees, and organic soils that can burn deep and are hard to put out, have all helped the fire grow. Fire crews say the rising wind has made a direct attack unsafe at times.

    How far has the smoke from the fire travelled?

    Smoke has travelled hundreds of kilometres south, reaching north-east Tasmania and as far as St Helens, carried by northerly winds. Several roads on Flinders Island have been closed because smoke has cut visibility, and smoke is now coming down the Tasmanian coast.

    Will rain help put the Flinders Island fire out?

    A band of rain and thunderstorms sweeping across Australia later in the week is expected to help reduce or extinguish the fire from Friday into Saturday. That same system also brings a risk of severe thunderstorms and flooding in parts of eastern and south-eastern Australia, so the relief is not risk-free and forecasts can change.
